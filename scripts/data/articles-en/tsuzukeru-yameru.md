Should you keep your current job, or call it a day? Should you let go here of a hobby or side work you have kept up for years? Should you settle a relationship that feels off soon? Has only the decision to "quit" stayed undecided, put off for months, even years?

Let me say this first: the goal of this article is not to push your back with "quit." The goal is to separate attachment to the past from your true feelings. Whether you keep going or quit, the aim is to arrive at a decision you can describe not as "because I worked this hard to get here," but as "because the self I am becoming wants to choose it." Here is how to sort that out.

## POINT 01 Why is "quitting" so hard?

Beginning something comes with forward-looking energy, but quitting comes with a particular pain. One part of its true nature is what behavioral economics calls the "sunk cost" — attachment to a cost you have already paid and cannot recover. "I've spent this much time and money to get here — if I quit now, it will all be wasted." This feeling keeps justifying a present situation that feels off.

But think about it calmly. The time and money you poured in in the past will not come back whether you continue or not. All we can choose is "where to use the time and energy from here on." Past costs do not become material for judging the future. You may understand that in logic, but your feelings do not catch up. That is exactly why another ruler is needed.

That ruler is the "zero-base question." **If you were starting today from a state of having nothing, would you choose this once more?** With all the time you spent reset, would you want to start this job anew tomorrow? With no ties from the past at all, would you want to build this relationship from scratch? If you cannot answer right away, what you are clinging to now may not be the value of the thing itself, but your past self's effort.

## POINT 02 What the cards help you see

![Tarot card "Death." A skeletal knight on a white horse, and the rising sun](/assets/rider-waite/ar13-320.webp)

[Death](/cards/ar13.html), contrary to its name, is a card that stands for "an ending and a beginning." What it depicts is not destruction, but the closing of one chapter, and the sun rising beyond it. Ending something is also making room to begin something new. This card gently removes the assumption that "quitting = failure."

![Tarot card "Wheel of Fortune." A great wheel that keeps turning, and creatures in the four corners](/assets/rider-waite/ar10-320.webp)

[Wheel of Fortune](/cards/ar10.html) shows a great wheel that keeps turning. Things have a flow and a timing, and nothing stays in the same place forever, and nothing keeps falling forever either. When your current situation looks like "a reality that cannot be changed," this card tells you "that is only one scene in the flow." It is a perspective for looking at where you are in the flow, rather than fearing change itself.

![Tarot card "Seven of Pentacles." A figure gazing at the crops they have grown](/assets/rider-waite/pe07-320.webp)

[Seven of Pentacles](/cards/pe07.html) shows a figure gazing at the crops they have grown, standing still and looking back. The judgment of whether to keep going or quit is born not from momentum or emotion, but from a time of quietly checking "how was the harvest so far?" and "is there a prospect of more bearing fruit ahead?" Standing still is not running away.

## POINT 03 Keep the "process of being torn" in a decision log

Even if you answer the zero-base question once, what you're torn about returns again and again. "Maybe I should keep going after all" — your heart keeps swaying. That is where a decision log works. You keep the very state of "being torn over whether to keep going or quit" in a record, together with your reasons.

The way to record it is simple. Which feeling is stronger today? What is the reason? What material influenced the feeling, such as your condition or events? Build up a few weeks of this and look back, and you will see the pattern of how you are torn. "On tired days, I always want to quit." "On the day after I was praised, I want to keep going." When regularity appears in the waves of feeling, you become able to distinguish "is this feeling now my true feeling, or a temporary wave?"

Also, when you cannot quite decide "whether to quit" head-on, a pause with a deadline is also effective. You decide "at the end of the month three months from now, I will answer the zero-base question once more," and until that day, you keep going with all your strength. A pause with a boundary is different from putting things off. By deciding an end for the period of being torn itself, you can step out of the state of being dragged along by attachment, on and on.

And the greatest benefit of keeping a record is that it works the next time you are torn. If you decided to "keep going," the reason from that time remains. Even if what you're torn about comes again a few months later, because you have the ruler of "at that time I thought like this and kept going," you do not have to be torn again from zero. Conversely, on an anxious day after you decided to "quit," the reason you decided quietly supports you. ## POINT 04 Questions to ask yourself

Finally, in a quiet place, try asking yourself these questions. If you were starting from zero today, would you choose this once more? In one line, is your reason for continuing "because I want to," or "because I've come this far"? Which is larger: what you would lose if you quit, or what you keep losing while you continue? And once both hands are empty, what is the next thing you want to grasp?

There is no need to regret past choices. It is precisely because you threw yourself into it that seriously that your current experience and perspective grew. While thanking your past self, quietly letting go is a fine decision, and so is resolving yourself and facing it once more. The answer is not in the past. It is on the side of who you are becoming.
