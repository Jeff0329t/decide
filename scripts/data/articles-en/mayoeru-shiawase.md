Should you go, or not go? Should you buy, or not buy? There should be no great difference either way, and yet time passes without a decision. Have you ever felt down even about yourself for being torn — "how indecisive am I, to be torn over something like this"?

Let me say this first: that feeling of being torn is not proof that you lack the ability to decide. It is the opposite. The goal of this article is for you to learn how fortunate it is simply to be able to be torn, and to change how the decision you're torn over looks to you. And at the end, please take home a small method for deciding which one to let go of.

## POINT 01 You can be torn because both options are still alive

To begin with, when is it that people cannot be torn? The answer is simple: when they can only choose one of them. If your budget only covers one, the matter is settled before you compare. If only one can make the deadline, there is no room to worry. About things you cannot choose, there is simply no way to be torn.

Turn it around, and the fact that you are torn right now is proof that "you can choose either one." Whichever you choose, it would not be a disaster, and each has its own good points. That is why you cannot decide. It is not "I can't decide even though either would do," but "I can't decide because both are good." The length of time you spend torn is proportional to the quality of the options. I also sorted out the structure of indecisiveness in [Why you can't decide when you're indecisive, and how to find your way out](/articles/kimerarenai), but here we go one step further and look at "the circumstances of the side that gets to be torn."

Even so, being told that being torn is a happy thing may not make the pain any less painful. The true nature of that pain is not "not knowing the right answer." It is resistance to the fact that you want to keep holding both, but cannot keep holding both. To choose is to let go of the other one. A decision does not move forward because you keep avoiding the pain of letting go.

## POINT 02 What the cards help you see

![Tarot card "Two of Swords." A blindfolded woman holding two swords](/assets/rider-waite/sw02-320.webp)

[Two of Swords](/cards/sw02.html) shows a blindfolded woman crossing two swords and holding them, unable to move. This card reflects the very state of being torn. What I want you to notice is that she has not dropped either sword. Both swords are still in her hands. In other words, both options are still alive. While you are torn, you can still choose either one. Whether you see this figure as "helpless" or as "holding both" greatly changes how painful being torn feels.

![Tarot card "Two of Pentacles." A figure juggling two coins above the waves](/assets/rider-waite/pe02-320.webp)

[Two of Pentacles](/cards/pe02.html) shows a figure skillfully keeping two coins moving above the waves. Keeping hold of both is itself possible, but it takes continued energy. The cost of staying torn is this "tiredness of keeping them turning." Even a happy kind of being torn has an expiry date. When you grow tired of holding both, that may be a sign that "it is all right to set one of them down soon."

![Tarot card "The Lovers." An angel watching over a choice before two people](/assets/rider-waite/ar06-320.webp)

[The Lovers](/cards/ar06.html) is a card of choice. To choose is to take one side in light of your values, and let go of the other. What this card points to is not "which is correct," but "which self do you want to be?" I also wrote about the way of thinking that stops the search for the right answer in [Why searching for the right answer leaves you unable to decide](/articles/seikai-sagasu), but the standard for deciding which one to let go of is not gain and loss — it is your own values. Choose not the one that hurts less to let go of, but the one where you can explain to yourself later why you let it go. That is a way of deciding that leaves less regret behind.

## POINT 03 A record of having been torn makes the next decision you're torn over lighter

To turn "the happiness of being able to be torn" into something you can actually feel, it helps to keep a record of the times you were torn. What you were torn about, which one you chose, and how it turned out. As this record builds up, you notice a fact. Most of the decisions you were once so torn over would have been all right whichever way you chose.

A decision log is an accumulation of that evidence. The next time the same kind of decision you're torn over arrives, if you can think "last time, both were all right too. This time will probably be the same," the pain of letting go can be over while it is still small. The pain of being torn does not come from not knowing the right answer. It comes from the assumption that "once I choose, there is no taking it back." What dissolves that assumption is not logic, but your own past track record.

By keeping the process of being torn as well, "the time you spent torn" is not wasted — it becomes material for your future self. Everyday decisions you're torn over, like whether to go or not go, are exactly the kind of subject where a record shows its effect most easily. ## POINT 04 Common questions

Being torn may be a happy thing, but is it the same when a deadline is approaching? A decision you're torn over with a deadline cannot stay a happy one. Failing to choose at all becomes the worst choice in itself. In that case, it helps to decide your landing place in advance: "If I have not decided by the deadline, I will automatically go with this option." You are setting the limit on your own time to be torn.

Another question I often hear is, "Am I just being soft on myself because I can't decide?" You are not. Being able to find value in both is also the ability to see things from more than one side. The problem is not being torn itself. It is that time alone passes while you stay torn, and you receive the fruit of neither option.

Finally. Being able to be torn means you are living a life where you can choose. Someone in a place where they can only choose one might envy the very pain you feel. So that the happiness of being able to be torn does not end in pain alone: acknowledge once that both are alive, and then decide which one to let go of. The moment you decide, the time you spent torn turns into a good memory — "a time when I held both."
