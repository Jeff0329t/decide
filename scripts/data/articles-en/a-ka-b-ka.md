Change jobs, or stay? Take it on, or turn it down? Buy it now, or pass? You keep swaying between two options, and you've written out the pros and cons in a notebook. And still you can't decide. The comparison table is full, but your heart won't lean either way. Have you spent a night like that?

To say it up front: the reason you can't decide is not that you haven't thought it through enough. It's that the conditions you've lined up in your head and the standards your heart is asking for are measuring different things. The goal of this article is to bring the "what you really feel" that never shows up in a comparison table out onto the table.

## POINT 01 Why a comparison table still leaves you undecided

When you're torn between two options, most people compare the conditions. Salary, commute time, future prospects. That's the right procedure, but why is it that even when the table is complete, you often still can't decide?

The reason is that what people really want to know is not "which one is the better deal," but "the hardships of which path could I actually take on?" Gains and losses in terms of conditions are the head's work, but the feeling of "this hardship, I could carry" can only be measured by the heart. As long as your head and your heart are holding different rulers, no matter how many times you remake the table, that final push won't appear.

Another thing that's easy to overlook is the "cost of not deciding." While you're torn, time still passes, neither path gets prepared for, and only your feelings wear down. Putting off a decision is itself a choice. The structure of indecisiveness itself is also sorted out in [Why you can't decide when you're indecisive, and how to get out of it](/articles/kimerarenai). Here, we'll look at a method that works on the decision you're torn over once it's narrowed down to two options.

And one more thing: behind not being able to decide, there's also a fear of "regret over the path you didn't choose." If you choose one, the possibility of the other closes. That sense of loss is frightening, so you try to keep holding on to both without letting either go. But staying undecided is also a state where you receive the fruit of neither path. To get out of being torn between two options, it helps to first put the feeling of "I don't want to let go of either" itself into words and acknowledge it.

## POINT 02 Seeing it through the cards

![The tarot card "The Lovers." A figure choosing before two paths](/assets/rider-waite/ar06-320.webp)

[The Lovers](/cards/ar06.html) is a card of "choice," standing before two paths. The ruler this card points to is not gains and losses, but whether something matches your values. Not "which is the better deal," but "which way of living feels more like you." The core of the decision you're torn over between two options is usually here, and no matter how long you stare at a table of conditions, you can't answer this question from it.

![The tarot card "Two of Wands." A figure holding a globe, looking out over a distant view](/assets/rider-waite/wa02-320.webp)

[Two of Wands](/cards/wa02.html), in the figure of a person looking out over a distant view from a high place, represents the perspective of "comparing from the view a little further ahead." A two-way choice that's hard to call on immediate conditions alone can suddenly gain outline when you reframe it as "a year from now, which view do I want to be standing in?" Extending the time frame is a practical technique for finding what you really feel.

![The tarot card "Two of Pentacles." A figure juggling two coins on the waves](/assets/rider-waite/pe02-320.webp)

[Two of Pentacles](/cards/pe02.html) is the figure of a person who keeps juggling two coins on the waves. It represents a state where balance itself has become the goal, and you can't drop either one. Continuing to hold a two-way choice in both hands is, in fact, what drains the most energy. This card is a signal that makes you notice "the cost of continuing to sway."

## POINT 03 Using a two-card draw to give each path its own perspective

One method that works well for drawing out what you really feel in a two-way choice is the "two-card draw." For the self who chose option A and the self who chose option B, you give each one card's perspective.

The way to do it is simple. First, define the options clearly as "A = do ◯◯" and "B = don't do ◯◯." Next, draw one card for each path. Then, while looking at the words and imagery of the cards that came up, think about "if I chose this path, what would I end up facing?" If a card pointing to "challenge" comes up for changing jobs (A), and a card pointing to "building your footing" comes up for staying (B), what you ask yourself is not about luck. It's "the hardships of an unknown environment, and time spent building strength where you are now — which set of hardships do I feel I want to take on?"

Strangely enough, the moment you place a card's perspective in front of you, what you really feel — "actually, this hardship is the one I wanted to take on" — shows its face. The cards are not something that hits on the right answer; they're guide lines for imagining each path concretely.

If neither card stirs a strong reaction, that itself is also information. Maybe you're not really at the stage of deciding yet, or maybe you're looking for a third path that is neither A nor B. Don't be disappointed by a weak reaction; placing a provisional conclusion of "I don't have to decide just yet" is also a perfectly good way to use this. What matters is taking your feelings to a place that calculation of gains and losses in your head alone can never reach.

And once you've decided, leave just one line in your log about why you chose that one. The next time you're torn over a similar two-way choice, your past log will tell you "when I'm in a situation like this, what have I tended to put first?" One decision becomes material for the next decision. That's the greatest effect of continuing to keep a record.

## POINT 04 Questions to ask yourself

Finally, try asking yourself this. If you set all the conditions aside, which path is your heart leaning toward, even slightly? Between the "biggest hardship" of path A and the "biggest hardship" of path B, which do you want to take on? A year from now, which view do you want to be standing in? And how much more time swaying without deciding will you allow yourself?

A decision is not about choosing the right answer that won't fail. It's about deciding for yourself which set of hardships you will take on. When that resolve settles, being torn between two options ends. Leave one line about your reason, and move forward on the path you chose.
