Furniture or a gadget priced a little beyond your comfort zone, or a course for investing in yourself. You have the wish to have it, and yet your finger stops over the payment button — "Will I really get my money's worth?" "Won't this turn out to be a waste?" Have you had that experience? The reverse is also a familiar story: holding back on money, buying the cheaper compromise, then quickly stopping using it and regretting it — "I should have bought the one I really wanted from the start."

Your finger stopping is actually not a bad thing. Being able to pause before a large expense is also a sign of a healthy relationship with money. The problem is that after you pause, you have no material to check against. How to make that material is the theme of this article.

The goal of this article is not to learn saving techniques. It is to pause once before the decision you're torn over — spend, or save — and check "is this a way of using money that fits my values right now?" before you pay. Here is that way of thinking, as a habit.

## POINT 01 Thinking in "gain and loss" leaves you torn; thinking in "values" lets you decide

When we're torn over how to use money, we tend to try to judge only by gain-and-loss arithmetic — "is it a good deal?" "will I fail?" But the ruler of gain and loss is made of market prices and other people's evaluations. What decides whether that purchase is right for you is not whether the cost performance is good or bad, but one point: "does it fit the theme of my life right now?"

For example, the same ¥100,000 means something completely different as spending to restore your exhausted self than as spending for show. Conversely, saving itself is not the purpose, either. Holding back while "what for, and by when" stays vague is only putting anxiety off. Money saved in preparation for a job change in six months is an act of buying future options. On the other hand, money you keep saving out of vague anxiety may be a state where you are trying to buy peace of mind, and not managing to buy it. Even the same "savings" changes meaning when you hold it up against your values. For the full picture of when the spend-or-save judgment is hard, see the page [When you're torn over how to use money](/cards/worry-okane.html) as well.

## POINT 02 What the cards help you see

![Tarot card "Four of Pentacles." A figure holding a coin close, with coins on the head and feet as well](/assets/rider-waite/pe04-320.webp)

[Four of Pentacles](/cards/pe04.html) shows a figure holding a coin to the chest, unwilling to let it go. It stands for "guarding" and "the fear of letting go." When this card appears, what you're torn about may not be "do I really need it?" but "anxiety about the money decreasing" itself. Anxiety and necessity need to be thought about separately.

![Tarot card "Seven of Pentacles." A figure gazing at the crops they have grown, leaning on a staff](/assets/rider-waite/pe07-320.webp)

[Seven of Pentacles](/cards/pe07.html) shows a figure quietly gazing at the crops they have grown. It stands for "growing over time" and "judging the harvest." Not the price in front of you, but what that spending will bear over time. When you judge an investment in yourself, this perspective helps.

![Tarot card "Temperance." An angel pouring water between cups](/assets/rider-waite/ar14-320.webp)

[Temperance](/cards/ar14.html) shows an angel quietly pouring water between two cups. It stands for "balance," "harmony," and "the right measure." Spend or save is not a matter of one correct side, but of proportion. A season of spending with all your strength, and a season of saving firmly. It gives you the perspective of adjusting that blend to fit who you are now.

## POINT 03 Check the "purity of your reason for wanting it" with a card, and record the decision

Before a large purchase, try drawing one card while picturing the thing you are about to buy, and read its hint. Sometimes you can calm down — "this was not really a need; I was just trying to let off stress from work with an impulse buy" — and sometimes you can feel certain — "for me to move to the next stage now, this investment is necessary." What the card shows you is not the right answer, but the "purity" of your reason for wanting it.

And whether you decide to buy or decide not to buy, leave that reason in one line in your decision log. If "why I bought it" remains, then even if you stop using it, instead of blaming yourself with "it was a waste," you can look back and say "I needed it at that time." If your reason for not buying remains, you can avoid repeating the same decision you're torn over at every sale. The record that works especially well is the "reason for not buying." There are quite a few people who are torn over the same product every time a sale notification arrives. Instead of worrying from zero each time, you read back your past self's judgment. If you can see "at that time there was no occasion to use it. Nothing has changed now," what you're torn over ends in a few seconds. A record makes your next money decision faster and quieter. If you want to sort out the decision you're torn over about shopping itself, see [How to think when you're torn over whether to buy](/articles/kaimono-mayou) as well.

Once you start paying only after checking your own sense of acceptance, your satisfaction with shopping rises surprisingly. Not high or low, but whether you can say "I chose it by my own values." That is the key that untangles what you're torn over about money.

## POINT 04 Questions to ask yourself

Finally, the next time you face a large expense, try asking yourself these questions. Is this a way of using money that fits the theme of my life right now? Is my reason for wanting to buy it that I need it, or that I want to fill anxiety or stress? Conversely, is my reason for wanting to save it that I have a purpose, or simply that I am afraid of it decreasing? How is the self of one year from now likely to judge this expense?

Money is something you use not by the amount, but by your sense of acceptance. Pause just once before you pay, and hold it up against your own values. That small habit leads to a way of using money with less regret.
