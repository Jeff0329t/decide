At night, in bed, you open your phone and find yourself looking at photos of the car you want again. You run the quote simulator, then close it. You open the grade comparison table, then close that too. The wish to buy it is real, but when you think about tax, insurance, inspections, and parking fees, your hand stops. How many weeks has it been since you started comparing?

Let me say this first: the goal of this page is not "learning how to get a good deal." The goal is to be able to say, whether you buy or not, "that choice fit my life." Regret about a car does not come from the car itself. It comes from deciding without looking at how you will actually use it and what it will cost to keep.

## POINT 01 When you're torn over a car, it's a tug-of-war between "I want it" and "can I keep paying for it?"

The cost of a car is not decided by the purchase price alone. Even with a compact car priced at ¥2,500,000, running costs come to roughly ¥400,000 to ¥600,000 a year. Automobile tax is ¥25,000 to ¥45,000 a year for a standard car (¥10,800 for a kei car), voluntary insurance is ¥60,000 to ¥100,000 a year, and the vehicle inspection is ¥100,000 to ¥150,000 every two years. In a city, parking is ¥10,000 to ¥30,000 a month, and fuel adds up the more you drive. If you take out a loan, a fixed amount leaves your account every month for the five years of repayment.

How you pay is also part of what you're torn about. How much to put down, and whether to add bonus payments. A residual-value loan that makes the monthly payment look light leaves a large payment, or a decision about switching cars, waiting for you when the contract ends. If you choose only by the low monthly amount, you are passing the decision you are torn over now on to yourself a few years from now. On the other hand, if you put too much down and your savings grow thin, you cannot respond to the kinds of expenses that are unique to a car, like breakdowns and repairs. It is a picture of the total, including "the money that remains after you buy."

Look at it from how often you will use it, and the picture changes. If you only drive two or three times a month on weekends, a car share or rental car may cover it for around ¥100,000 a year. For someone who commutes every day, someone who drives children around, or someone for whom drives are at the center of daily life, the same car means something completely different.

So the material that untangles what you're torn about is not a comparison table of models. It is two things: what the "total for one year" comes to, and the concreteness of how you will use it — "with whom, to where, and how much will you drive?" Once you can say these two things, you start to see how to settle the tug-of-war between wanting it and reality.

## POINT 02 What the cards help you see

![Tarot card "The Chariot." A chariot moving forward, guided by two sphinxes](/assets/rider-waite/ar07-320.webp)

[The Chariot](/cards/ar07.html) shows a figure moving forward, holding the reins of two sphinxes. Its keywords are "will," "moving forward," and "control." It is a card that reflects the wish for a car itself, and it gives you a perspective that does not deny the feeling, while reminding you that you are the one holding the reins. Not buying on momentum, and not giving up by holding back, either — you set the conditions, your budget and your use, and move forward yourself. It reminds you of that stance. The Chariot is not a figure charging ahead by force. It is the skill of turning two different impulses in the same direction. Rather than making the impulse of "I want it" fight the impulse of "I want to protect the household budget," you move forward with both as parts of yourself. Setting the conditions is how you do that.

![Tarot card "Four of Pentacles." A figure holding a coin tightly](/assets/rider-waite/pe04-320.webp)

[Four of Pentacles](/cards/pe04.html) shows a figure holding a coin close and not letting go. It stands for "ownership," "attachment," and "guarding." In a decision about a car, it appears as anxiety about running costs, or as the feeling that you do not want to break into your savings. Is what you are holding on to peace of mind about the future, or is it pride — "I should at least have a car"? Once you see what it is you want to protect, how you handle the anxiety changes.

![Tarot card "Four of Wands." People celebrating a harvest under a garland of flowers](/assets/rider-waite/wa04-320.webp)

[Four of Wands](/cards/wa04.html) shows people celebrating under a garland of flowers, and stands for "stability," "celebration," and "a place to return to." A car brings more than a way to get around. Family trips, luggage on the way home, weekend outings. It is a perspective that puts a value on scenes from your life. It brings "the joy of a life with this car," which never appears in a calculation of running costs alone, back onto the table as material for your decision.

## POINT 03 Keep a record of your reasons and conditions for buying

Because a decision about a car is a big decision you make only once in several years, a record helps. If you keep the conditions you are working with now — your budget limit, your monthly running-cost limit, the uses you cannot give up — you can return to your own standards when a salesperson's proposal or a limited color tempts you and your feelings sway.

The same holds after you buy. If you keep the actual running costs and the moments when you felt "I'm glad I bought it," then at the next purchase in five or ten years, you can decide from your track record rather than from a feeling. And if you decide not to buy this time, if you leave one line about why you passed, you can avoid repeating the same decision you're torn over again and again. If you keep it together with how you were when you drew the cards, you can look back later and separate your feelings from the conditions — "that day I was impatient," "today my feet are on the ground."

If you have a family, it also matters to share those conditions with them. A car decided by one person alone becomes a small seed of discontent every time a running-cost bill arrives. If the conditions are put into words and kept, "what you prioritized when you chose" can be shared within the family too, and the decision does not become one person's burden. Conditions put into words last far longer than a decision you carry alone in your head. ## POINT 04 Questions to ask yourself

Finally, try asking yourself these questions, with the car you want in mind. Can you picture concretely the scenes where you are riding in that car? How many times a week, with whom, and to where? Can you say, without hesitation, the total for one year once you add the loan and the running costs together? Does it include parking? How much of your use could a car share cover instead? Five years from now, could you say that payment was "worth it"? And if you decide not to buy, can you say why in one sentence?

It is fine if the answer is "buy" or "don't buy." What matters is the feeling that you decided by your own conditions, not by longing or impatience. A decision with that feeling behind it does not waver later, even when you look at a running-cost statement. The length of time you spend torn is not a failure. The bigger the expense, the more time you need to put the conditions into words. The one holding the wheel is always you.
