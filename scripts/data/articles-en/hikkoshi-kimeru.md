Your favorites on the property site have grown again. You do have complaints about your current home. The smallness, the sunlight, the distance of the commute, the neighbor's noise echoing at night. But the renewal period arrives without a decisive reason, and you put it off with "another two years here is fine." Every time you catch sight of a moving notice or a topic about a new life, only your feelings get stirred up, little by little. The reason a moving decision is heavy is that, on top of "whether to do it or not," there are three things to decide piled together, including "when" and "where to."

To say it up front: the goal of this page is not to judge "whether you should move." The goal is to untangle the three piled-up decisions so that you can make only the decision you need now. If you stay with a vague dissatisfaction of "someday I want to move," the same decision you're torn over will return at every renewal.

## POINT 01 The moving decision gets lighter when you split it into three

The first is "whether to do it or not." The key is what's really behind your complaints about your current home. Is the rent high, is the room small, does the environment not suit you, is the commute far? And is that complaint really a "problem with the home," or is a problem with work busyness or your life rhythm just looking like a complaint about the home? Once you know this, you can see whether moving will be the solution.

The second is "timing." The cost of moving changes a lot by timing. In the busy season of March, it can cost ¥100,000 to ¥150,000 even for a single person, while in a quieter period it may be settled for ¥50,000 to ¥80,000. For total upfront costs, a guideline is four to six months' rent, including deposit, key money, and brokerage fees. On rent of ¥80,000, that's ¥320,000 to ¥480,000. Whether it's before or after paying the renewal fee (one month's rent) also changes the meaning of moving.

The third is "place." If your commute shortens by 30 minutes one way, you get back one hour a day round trip, and on weekdays alone, roughly 250 hours a year. Even if the rent goes up by ¥10,000 a month, you can think of it as an expense that "buys time." If you think of the three separately, you can decide only "whether to do it or not" now, and leave timing and place as conditions. The lightening of the decision comes from that breakdown.

One more important thing: even when you decide "not to move," conditions are needed. Part of your dissatisfaction can be resolved without moving. Reviewing storage, rearranging furniture, a bold declutter or a small repair. It's the work of separating dissatisfaction that can be resolved with a few tens of thousands of yen of ingenuity from dissatisfaction that can't be resolved unless you change the home itself. If you first clear away what can be resolved without moving, the judgment of "whether to do it or not" becomes much more accurate.

## POINT 02 Seeing it through the cards

![The tarot card "Wheel of Fortune." A large wheel that keeps turning](/assets/rider-waite/ar10-320.webp)

[Wheel of Fortune](/cards/ar10.html), in the figure of a large wheel that keeps turning, represents "a turning point," "timing," and "how things come around." Moving tends to come with outer milestones such as a contract renewal, a job change, or a change in the family. It doesn't mean leave it to fate; it's the perspective of riding, by your own choice, the timing when the wheel turns. It makes you check whether a milestone has arrived and you're staying still, or whether you're trying to rush into moving when there is no milestone.

![The tarot card "Eight of Wands." Eight wands flying through the sky](/assets/rider-waite/wa08-320.webp)

[Eight of Wands](/cards/wa08.html), in the figure of eight wands flying vigorously through the sky, represents "rapid development," "speed," and "moving forward all at once." Property hunting actually moves fast, and it's not rare for a good room to fill up within a few days from viewing to application. While it points to the speed needed after you've decided, it's also a caution against "deciding on momentum alone." It's a perspective for telling apart situations where speed is needed from situations where you should leave it overnight.

![The tarot card "The Fool." A young person stepping out lightly with a single bundle](/assets/rider-waite/ar00-320.webp)

[The Fool](/cards/ar00.html) is the figure of a young person walking lightly along a cliff edge with a single bundle. It represents "beginnings," "freedom," and "lightness." It's a card that symbolizes a step toward a new daily life, not an extension of your current one. However, it does not affirm being unplanned. Lightness on top of having your upfront costs and conditions in order is what turns moving into a positive decision.

## POINT 03 Log what frustrates you about your current home

The decision you're torn over about moving untangles through records. Woken in the middle of the night by the neighbor's noise, a room so cold in winter it was hard, coming home exhausted from the commute. If you note moments like those briefly each time, the feeling of "somehow I want to get out" turns into a concrete list of conditions. That list becomes, just as it is, the checklist for your next home.

Even after you've moved, records keep working. Did that complaint resolve in the new home, and what didn't resolve? When a review remains, "what I really ask of a home" becomes words. Years later, at a move after that, it will be more reliable material for judgment than a condition search on a property site.

Records of days you went to view properties are also worth keeping. Impressions from viewings mix together once you get home, and what was good about which room becomes vague. If you leave "good points, points that concerned me, rent and upfront costs" briefly on the same day, you can compare multiple properties with the same ruler. Much of the reason you can't decide is that the material for comparison has melted away inside your memory. If you leave it together with your state when you drew a card, you can also look back on whether the complaint was fact, or that day's tiredness. Sorting things out when you can't decide about moving is also gathered on the page [When you can't decide about moving](/cards/worry-hikkoshi.html).

## POINT 04 Questions to ask yourself

Finally, try asking toward your current home. Can you name three complaints, concretely? Can those complaints only be resolved by moving? Do you grasp the total of upfront costs and the effect on your savings? How many months are left until the contract renewal? If you were to buy back commute time, how much per month could you add on top of the rent? Can you concretely picture a weekday morning in the place you'd move to? And if you decide "I'll watch how it goes for another two years," can you say that too, not as putting it off, but as one decision, in your own words?

Deciding where to live is deciding the shape of your everyday life. Whether to do it or not, timing, place. If you can decide even one of those in your own words, the decision you're torn over has certainly become lighter.
